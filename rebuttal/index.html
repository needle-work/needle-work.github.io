1. What if bridges don't match the remaining actions?

Reviewers 4XGn and voED

Our training method does not assume an exact connection between a bridge and the target's remaining actions. We see that the verifier plays an important role in filtering out harmful errors, and our training method ensures we're able to learn well in spite of any remaining imprecision. Training windows stop action supervision at the bridge endpoint (Figure 3 of the main paper). The target's remaining actions are learned separately, paired with their own recorded observations. Appending those actions directly to the bridge reduces downstream success on all three tasks. We also test whether a closed-loop policy can continue from bridge landings.

2. Which bridge errors hurt, and does the verifier remove them?

Reviewers 4XGn and voED

We analyze two different types of bridge errors that occur in our dataset: pose error, where the arm lands >5cm from the target location, and grasp error, where the gripper drops the object during the bridge. We find that grasp errors hurt the policy more, lowering its performance below DP. Our verifier keeps far fewer of these errors than a value function.

6. Are the three categories needed?

Reviewer 4XGn

The three stages serve different purposes, and combining them gives the highest mean success in the reported subset comparison. We ablate the effects of including different subsets of the three stages, and find that while most subsets improve above DP, including all 3 still helps the most.

The three categories are the three ways a useful target can relate to a source, given that the target must lie on a successful trajectory: on the source's own successful trajectory (S1), on another successful trajectory from a successful source (S2), or on a successful trajectory from a failed source (S3). All three use the same retrieval, IDM, verifier, and selection rule. In earlier development, to test whether the categories themselves are needed, we replaced them with a single ranking of all accepted bridges by verifier score. This recovered part of the gain but fell short of the full method. The key role of the categories is therefore how they allocate the bridge budget: each category supplies a different kind of connection, and the ablation above shows that no single category, and no subset we tested, matches using all three.

7. How are target states retrieved?

Reviewer ak1N

Retrieving targets for one Square source state

  1. Input: recorded observations, horizon H, candidate budgets
  2. Calibrate distance cutoff r from recorded H-step transitions.
  3. For each source s:
  4. collect eligible targets across trajectories;
  5. retain targets within cosine distance r of s;
  6. sample up to k targets round-robin across distance bins;
  7. propose bridges with the IDM, and keep those the verifier accepts.
  8. Output: verified bridges for policy training.

φ: frozen visual features the policy is trained on. Cosine distance d(a, b) = 1 − cos(φ(a), φ(b)).

Indeed, exhaustive sampling would provide the broadest coverage of candidate pairs. However, generating and verifying bridges for all O(N2) source–target pairs is expensive, so we design our sampling procedure to distribute a limited budget across sources and visual distances.

First, we exclude target states that cannot provide the required step saving (only S1 and S2 categories, rather than failure-to-success recovery). Retrieval then uses cosine similarity in the frozen visual features the policy is trained on. Since the inverse dynamics model has a fixed horizon of H, we calibrate a visual-distance cutoff from recorded observations H steps apart. For S2 and S3, eligible target states are pooled across trajectories. We do not select a target trajectory first; target states are selected directly from all eligible states. We sample round-robin across visual-distance bins for each source, then allocate the overall candidate budget round-robin across source states with available targets.

8. What does offline rewriting cost?

Reviewer 4XGn

GPU wall-clock for full pipeline. Our method requires just a one-time step to rewrite the offline dataset, and does not impact the actual duration of policy training, nor inference time. All times measured on RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell GPUs. The prepared corpus can be reused across downstream seeds and configurations, so stitching is a one-time cost.

How the cost scales. Rewriting time is dominated by scoring candidates with the IDM and verifier, and it grows linearly with the number of candidates (a least-squares fit gives R² = 0.998 on Can and 0.999 on Square, over 5k–200k candidates). Each source receives a fixed candidate budget, so the cost grows linearly with dataset size rather than with the number of source–target pairs. The GPU time per candidate (eight IDM samples plus verification) is similar across tasks: 7–15 ms on Robomimic and 7–9 ms on the real-robot data.
